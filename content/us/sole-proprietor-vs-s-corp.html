How the comparison works

As a sole proprietor (or a single-member LLC, taxed the same way by default), your whole profit bears self-employment tax (15.3% on 92.35% of it, Social Security capped) and income tax. As an S corporation, the business pays you a salary: Social Security and Medicare apply to that salary only, on both the employer and employee side (7.65% each, Social Security capped at $184,500), plus federal unemployment tax (0.6% on the first $7,000 when the full state credit applies). The rest of the profit passes through to you without self-employment tax.

Before you switch

  • Pay yourself a reasonable salary. With too low a salary, the IRS can treat your distributions as wages, with the payroll taxes due on them.
  • Count the S corp's own costs: running payroll, filing Form 1120-S, and any state taxes or fees. Enter them above to see the net effect.
  • Look beyond this year's tax: a lower salary means lower Social Security earnings on your record, and SEP-IRA contributions are based on your W-2 salary.
  • Check your state's rules: state income tax and state fees aren't in this comparison.
