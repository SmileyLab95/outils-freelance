The math behind your rate

Hourly rate = (profit needed + expenses) ÷ billable hours × (1 + safety margin)

The profit needed is the amount that leaves you your target after self-employment tax (15.3% on 92.35% of profit), federal income tax at 2026 rates, and your state estimate. It is found exactly, not with a flat percentage, so the rate stays right at any income level.

Billable hours are your working weeks times your weekly hours, minus the share of time you spend on unbillable work. For most freelancers, that share is the biggest blind spot.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate my freelance hourly rate?
Start from the yearly take-home pay you want, add the taxes you will owe on your profit (self-employment tax and income tax), add your business expenses, then divide by the hours you can actually bill in a year. This calculator does each step for you with 2026 tax rules.
How many billable hours does a freelancer have per year?
Fewer than an employee is paid for. With 4 weeks off, 10 holidays and 40 working hours a week, you work about 1,840 hours, but part of that time goes to marketing, admin, invoicing and learning, which you can't bill. By default the calculator counts 30% unbillable time, which leaves 1,288 billable hours: change it to match your own year.
Why is a freelance rate so much higher than an employee's hourly wage?
Because the rate has to pay for things an employer normally covers: the employer half of Social Security and Medicare, health insurance, retirement, paid time off, equipment, software, and the hours spent finding clients. A common rule of thumb is to charge at least 1.5 to 2 times the hourly wage of an equivalent salaried job.
Should I charge by the hour or by the project?
Hourly works well when the scope is unclear. For well-defined work, a project price based on your hourly rate times the estimated hours, plus a buffer of 15 to 25%, rewards you for working efficiently.
