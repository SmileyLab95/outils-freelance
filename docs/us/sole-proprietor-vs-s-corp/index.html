How the comparison works

As a sole proprietor (or a single-member LLC, taxed the same way by default), your whole profit bears self-employment tax (15.3% on 92.35% of it, Social Security capped) and income tax. As an S corporation, the business pays you a salary: Social Security and Medicare apply to that salary only, on both the employer and employee side (7.65% each, Social Security capped at $184,500), plus federal unemployment tax (0.6% on the first $7,000 when the full state credit applies). The rest of the profit passes through to you without self-employment tax.

Before you switch

  • Pay yourself a reasonable salary. With too low a salary, the IRS can treat your distributions as wages, with the payroll taxes due on them.
  • Count the S corp's own costs: running payroll, filing Form 1120-S, and any state taxes or fees. Enter them above to see the net effect.
  • Look beyond this year's tax: a lower salary means lower Social Security earnings on your record, and SEP-IRA contributions are based on your W-2 salary.
  • Check your state's rules: state income tax and state fees aren't in this comparison.

Frequently asked questions

When does an S corp save tax?
The profit an S corporation passes through to you isn't subject to self-employment tax: you pay Social Security and Medicare (both halves, plus federal unemployment tax) only on the salary it pays you. Three things shrink the saving: the S corp's own costs (payroll, a separate tax return, state taxes and fees), a smaller QBI deduction below the income threshold (your salary isn't qualified business income), and, on profit above the Social Security wage base, a saving limited to the Medicare part.
What is a reasonable salary?
The IRS requires an S corporation to pay a shareholder who works in the business reasonable compensation before any distribution, and it can treat distributions as wages if the salary is too low. There's no formula: your training, duties, time spent and what similar businesses pay for the same work all count. The calculator doesn't suggest a salary.
Is an LLC the same as an S corp?
No. A single-member LLC is taxed like a sole proprietorship by default: its profit goes on Schedule C and bears self-employment tax. It's taxed as an S corporation only if it elects to, by filing Form 2553.
Does an S corp change my QBI deduction?
Yes, both ways. Your salary isn't qualified business income, so below the taxable income threshold the deduction is smaller than as a sole proprietor. Above the threshold, the deduction is limited to 50% of the W-2 wages the business pays, which includes your salary: an S corp can then keep part of a deduction a sole proprietor would lose.
What else changes with an S corp?
Social Security benefits are based on your covered earnings: a lower salary means lower earnings on your record. Retirement contributions to a SEP-IRA are based on your W-2 salary rather than your self-employment earnings. Health insurance for a more-than-2% shareholder goes through your W-2. The corporation also runs payroll and files its own returns.
What isn't included?
State income taxes and fees (some states tax S corporations), state unemployment tax, payroll and accounting costs (enter them as extra costs), health insurance and retirement contributions. For married filing separately, the single tax brackets are used: check the top brackets if your taxable income is high. The $400 minimum QBI deduction from 2026 is included; its details are still to be confirmed on IRS forms.
