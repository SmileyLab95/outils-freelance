    What these terms cover

    • For all your clients: who you are and how to reach you, how the contract is formed from your quote, prices and payment, the date by which a client needs your invoice, freelance protection laws, how you carry out the services, copyright, liability, ending the contract, disputes and the governing law.
    • With business clients: a period to check deliverables, the right to pause work on an unpaid invoice, a warranty disclaimer and a limit of liability, and the courts of your state.
    • With consumers: a reminder that their rights under their state’s consumer protection laws are not limited, and no limit of liability, warranty disclaimer, deemed acceptance, “entire agreement” term or exclusive choice of courts. For sales made at their home or elsewhere away from your place of business, the three-day right to cancel, with the official notice of cancellation in an annex.

    How to use them

    • Send your terms with every quote, as a PDF, before the client accepts it, and ask them to sign the quote or confirm their acceptance by email: this is what makes the terms part of the contract. An electronic signature is valid under the federal E-SIGN Act. Prepare your quotes with the invoice and estimate generator.
    • Describe in each quote the services, their price, how you charge, the payment date and the timetable.
    • Keep each dated version of your terms and a copy of every accepted quote: the terms in force on the day a quote is accepted apply to that contract.
    • For a large project, add a freelance contract signed by both parties.

    Freelance Isn’t Free: what your quote must say

    Some states and cities require a written contract when a client hires a freelancer. The duty is the client’s, but your accepted quote and these terms can be that contract if they contain what the law asks for:

    • New York State (General Business Law, article 44-A, contracts of $800 or more, alone or with the same client over the previous 120 days): both parties’ names and mailing addresses, an itemized list of the services, their value, the rate and method of pay, the payment date (or how it is set) and the date by which you must send your list of services or invoice for the client’s processing deadlines.
    • New York City ($800 or more, counting contracts with the same client over 120 days): the work, the pay and the payment date.
    • Illinois ($500 or more over 120 days): both parties’ names and contact information, the products and services, the rate and method of pay, the payment date and the dates of the services.

    In these places, a client must pay by the date in the contract or, if there is none, within 30 days after you finish, and must not retaliate against you for using your rights; in New York State, a client cannot make you accept less to be paid on time. Other states and cities have adopted similar laws: see the Freelance Isn’t Free guide.

    Selling at a client’s home

    The Federal Trade Commission’s Cooling-Off Rule (16 CFR Part 429) applies when a consumer agrees to buy your services at their home for $25 or more, or at another place that is not your place of business (their workplace, a hotel, a convention center, a fair) for $130 or more, after you have presented your services in person, even at their invitation. It does not apply to business clients, to sales made entirely by mail or phone, or to a repair or maintenance visit they asked for, among other exceptions. When it applies:

    • When they agree to buy, tell them about their right to cancel and give them a copy of the contract or a receipt showing the date, your name and your address, with the cancellation statement in bold type of at least 10 points next to their signature.
    • Give them the notice of cancellation in two copies, both completed: print the annex twice. Enter the date of the sale in the form (“Your clients”) to fill in its date and the deadline: midnight of the third business day, not counting Sundays and federal holidays.
    • Use the same language as your presentation (for example Spanish), and give paper copies unless they agreed to receive the documents electronically as the E-SIGN Act requires.
    • Do not start the work before the deadline. If they cancel, refund every payment within 10 business days. Never ask them to waive the right.
    • Your state’s law on home sales still applies and may give consumers more rights.

    What this generator does not cover

    These terms are for services provided by a business established in the United States. They do not suit the sale of goods, subscriptions and contracts that renew automatically, financial services, licensed professions, construction and home improvement work, or clients based outside the US, and they do not include each state’s own consumer protection rules. Have your terms reviewed by an attorney if your work carries significant risks. If a client does not pay, see the small claims court guide.

    Frequently asked questions

    Do freelancers need terms and conditions in the US?
    No federal law requires them, but some states and cities require a written contract with a freelancer: in New York State and New York City from $800 (alone or with the same client over 120 days) and in Illinois from $500 over 120 days. Your accepted quote and your terms can form that contract.
    Are terms accepted by email or e-signature binding?
    Yes. Under the federal E-SIGN Act, a contract or signature cannot be denied legal effect just because it is electronic. If a law requires you to give a consumer information in writing, you can give it electronically only with their prior consent.
    Can my clients cancel a contract?
    Consumers who agree to buy at their home ($25 or more) or at another place that is not your place of business ($130 or more) can cancel until midnight of the third business day under the FTC Cooling-Off Rule, and you must refund them within 10 business days. Business clients and sales made entirely by mail or phone are not covered; state laws may add rights.
    Can I charge interest on late invoices?
    Only if your contract says so: no federal law sets a late payment rate between private businesses, and the rate must respect your state’s usury and late-fee rules. In New York State and New York City, a client who pays late can also owe you double damages under the Freelance Isn’t Free Act.
    Who owns the work I create for a client?
    You do, unless you transfer the copyright: a transfer is valid only in a writing signed by you (17 U.S.C. 204(a)). These terms assign the rights once you are paid in full; sign the quote or contract that refers to them.
