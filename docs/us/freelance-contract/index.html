    What every freelance contract should cover

    • Scope and deliverables: the more precise, the fewer arguments about what's included.
    • Fees, deposit and payment terms: a 30% deposit protects your cash flow; a late fee clause gives you leverage. Follow up with a payment reminder when needed.
    • Revisions: a set number of rounds keeps the project from dragging on.
    • Ownership: assigning the IP only once you're paid in full is your best protection against non-payment.
    • Termination: notice, and payment for the work already done.

    Before the project is signed, protect your discussions with an NDA.

    Frequently asked questions

    Do I need a written contract as a freelancer?
    Often, yes by law. New York State, New York City and Illinois require a written contract for freelance work worth $800 or more ($500 in Illinois), alone or combined with work for the same client over 120 days, and other cities have similar rules. Even where it isn't required, a contract settles scope, payment and ownership before problems arise.
    Who owns the work: me or my client?
    You do, until you transfer it in writing. Under US copyright law, work by an independent contractor isn't “work made for hire” except in a few narrow categories with a signed agreement. This template uses a written assignment that takes effect when you've been paid in full, which also gives you leverage if a client doesn't pay.
    Can I charge late fees?
    Yes, if your contract says so and the rate respects your state's usury and penalty rules. 1.5% per month is common. Freelance protection laws also give you double damages when a covered client pays late. See the late fee calculator.
    How do I avoid being misclassified as an employee?
    The contract helps, but what matters is how the work is done: you control how and when you work, use your own tools, can work for other clients and invoice as a business. States such as California apply a stricter ABC test.
    Is this contract legally binding?
    Yes, once both parties sign it, including with an electronic signature. It is a general template for business-to-business projects, not legal advice: for a large project, work abroad or sensitive data, have a lawyer review it.
