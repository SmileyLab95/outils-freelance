Late fees: what you need to know

  • Agree on it first. A late fee is enforceable when the client accepted it before the work: put it in your contract or proposal, and repeat it on every invoice.
  • Stay reasonable. States regulate interest rates and penalties differently. Adding “or the maximum rate permitted by law, if lower” keeps your clause safe.
  • Know your protections. In New York (city and state), Illinois, California and several cities, freelancer protection laws require written contracts above a threshold and payment within 30 days of completing the work when no date was agreed.

Information only, not legal advice. For large amounts, talk to a lawyer or consider small claims court.

Frequently asked questions

Can a freelancer charge a late fee?
Yes, if your client agreed to it before the work started, usually in your contract, proposal or terms printed on the invoice. No federal law sets a late fee on invoices between businesses, so a fee you add after the fact is hard to enforce.
What is a normal late fee on an invoice?
1% to 1.5% per month on the overdue balance (12% to 18% a year) is common, or a flat fee such as $25 to $50 for small invoices. Some states cap interest rates or late charges, so keep the rate reasonable and add “or the maximum rate permitted by law, if lower” to your terms.
How do I calculate 1.5% per month interest?
Multiply the overdue amount by 1.5% for each full month late, or by 18% ÷ 365 for each day late if your terms say interest accrues daily. On a $2,000 invoice paid 45 days late: 2,000 × 18% × 45 ÷ 365 = $44.38.
Should I always charge the late fee?
Charging it consistently makes your terms credible, but waiving it once for a good client who pays right away can be a smart gesture. Mention the fee in your reminders before the invoice is due so it never comes as a surprise.
