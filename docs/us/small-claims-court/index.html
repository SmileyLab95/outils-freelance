    How a small claims case works

    1. Final demand. Send a last written demand with the amount, a deadline and what you'll do next: the payment reminder generator writes it.
    2. Check the limit and the court. Usually in the state, and the county, where your client lives or does business: each state's rules say where you can file.
    3. File your claim and pay the filing fee, which depends on the state, the county and the amount.
    4. Serve your client. The claim must reach your client in the way your state's rules require.
    5. The hearing. Bring your contract or accepted quote, the invoice, your reminders and any reply from your client. Some courts offer mediation first, and in some it's mandatory.
    6. Collect. If your client still doesn't pay after the judgment, ask the court how to enforce it, for example by garnishing wages or levying a bank account.

    Good to know

    • Interest and late fees: claim what your contract provides. The late fee calculator works it out.
    • Freelance protection laws: in New York, California and a few other places, freelance laws add remedies, such as double damages for late payment in New York: see the Freelance Isn't Free Act guide.
    • Don't wait: the time limit to sue depends on your state and on whether your contract was written.

    Information for guidance only, from each state's court and legislature pages. Limits change: check with the court clerk before you file. For a large or complex claim, talk to a lawyer or your state's legal aid.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can I take a client to small claims court for an unpaid invoice?
    Yes. Every state has a small claims court (or a court with a simplified procedure) for money claims up to a limit, designed for people who represent themselves. Send a final demand letter first, then file in the state, and usually the county, where your client lives or does business: each state's rules say where you can file.
    What is the small claims limit in my state?
    It ranges from $2,500 in Kentucky to $25,000 in Delaware and Tennessee. Some limits depend on the court or the county (New York, North Carolina, Pennsylvania), and Georgia and Wisconsin raise theirs for claims filed from January 1, 2027. Many states don't count interest and court costs towards the limit.
    Does it matter if I have an LLC or a corporation?
    Sometimes. In California, a business entity can claim up to $6,250 instead of $12,500, and in Washington $5,000 instead of $10,000; in New York, a corporation files a commercial claim. Some states require a company to have a lawyer (Illinois, the District of Columbia, and New Jersey above $5,000), while others don't allow lawyers at all (Michigan, Nebraska). A sole proprietor sues as an individual.
    Do I need a lawyer for small claims court?
    No: small claims courts are designed for people who represent themselves. Some states don't allow lawyers, or allow them only with the judge's permission.
    How long do I have to sue for an unpaid invoice?
    It depends on your state's statute of limitations, which is often different for written and oral contracts. Check your state's law, and don't wait: the older the debt, the harder it is to collect.
