Which plan should you choose?

  • Solo 401(k) if you want to save as much as possible on a moderate income, want Roth contributions, or are 50 or older. It needs a bit more paperwork (Form 5500-EZ once assets exceed $250,000).
  • SEP-IRA if you want the simplest setup, decide late (you can open it until your filing deadline), or plan to hire employees later and cover them.

Contributions lower your income tax, so your quarterly payments can be lower too: check with the quarterly tax calculator and how much to set aside.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between a SEP-IRA and a solo 401(k)?
Both let a self-employed person contribute as the “employer”: up to 20% of net earnings from self-employment. A solo 401(k) also allows “employee” deferrals of up to $24,500 in 2026, plus catch-up contributions from age 50, so it lets you save much more at low and middle incomes. A SEP-IRA is simpler to open and run.
How much can I put in a SEP-IRA in 2026?
20% of your net earnings from self-employment (net profit minus half of your self-employment tax), up to $72,000. With $100,000 of net profit, that's about $18,587.
How much can I put in a solo 401(k) in 2026?
Up to $24,500 as employee deferrals, plus 20% of net earnings as employer contributions, with a combined limit of $72,000. Catch-up contributions add $8,000 from age 50, or $11,250 between ages 60 and 63.
Do retirement contributions reduce self-employment tax?
No. They reduce your income tax (and usually state income tax), but self-employment tax is calculated before them. They also reduce the qualified business income deduction, which the calculator takes into account.
What are the deadlines?
A SEP-IRA can be opened and funded until your tax filing deadline, including extensions. For a solo 401(k), employer contributions can be made until the filing deadline; employee deferrals must be elected by year-end, and since 2023 a sole proprietor can open a new plan and make deferrals for the prior year until the original filing deadline.
