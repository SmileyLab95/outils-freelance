How a fixed-fee price is built

A fixed fee is agreed up front, whatever time the work actually takes. It starts from your time estimate for each task, plus a contingency buffer for revisions, meetings and waiting for approvals. That time, multiplied by your day rate, gives your fees; the expenses you include in the price (travel, purchases) are added on top.

Write in the quote what the price includes (deliverables, number of revision rounds) and what will be billed on top: that's what protects a fixed fee from scope creep. The invoice and quote generator turns it into a document, and the day rate converter shows your rate per hour, month or year.

Frequently asked questions

How do I price a fixed-fee project?
Estimate the time each task will take, add a buffer for the unexpected (revisions, meetings, delays), multiply by your rate and add any expenses included in the price. With 12 estimated days, a 10% buffer and a £400 day rate, the price is £5,280 before VAT.
How big should the contingency buffer be?
It depends on how clear the scope is and how often you've done this kind of project. The calculator starts at 10% as an example: raise it when the scope is vague or the client tends to add requests.
Should I ask for a deposit?
Nothing requires it, but a deposit when the client signs lowers the risk of not being paid and funds the start of the work. Agree on the amount and write it in your quote or contract.
A client gave me a budget: is it enough?
Enter it as the client's price: the calculator subtracts the expenses, divides the rest by your estimated time and compares the result with your day rate. A fixed fee only works if your time estimate is reliable.
