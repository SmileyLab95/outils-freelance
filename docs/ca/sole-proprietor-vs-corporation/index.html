How the comparison works

As a sole proprietor, your profit is taxed at your personal rates and bears both halves of the CPP (11.9% of earnings between $3,500 and $74,600, and 8% CPP2 on earnings from $74,600 to $85,000). Through a corporation (a Canadian-controlled private corporation), the profit left after your salary is taxed at the small business rate: 9% federal plus a provincial rate, on the first $500,000 (in Quebec, the provincial small business rate needs 5,500 paid hours a year; otherwise the 11.5% general rate applies). What you take out as dividends is taxed again in your hands, with a 15% gross-up and dividend tax credits that offset the corporate tax.

Both sides use the 2026 tax year: the corporation's year runs from January 1 to December 31, 2026. Ontario's small business rate falls from 3.2% to 2.2% on July 1, 2026 (2026 Ontario Budget), so the year's rate is prorated.

Before you incorporate

  • Make sure you're not an incorporated employee. If you'd be your client's employee without the corporation, it's a personal services business: no small business rate, and few deductions.
  • Think about your pension and RRSP. Dividends carry no CPP contributions and earn no pension; RRSP room comes from a salary (18% of it, up to the annual limit, for the following year), not from dividends.
  • Count the costs: incorporation, an accountant, the corporation's T2 return and annual filings. Enter them above to see the net effect.
  • Profit left in the corporation isn't tax-free: personal tax is due when it's paid out. Invested inside the corporation, its income is taxed differently, and passive income above $50,000 a year reduces the small business limit.
  • Paying dividends to family members can trigger the tax on split income, at the highest rate.

Frequently asked questions

Is it worth incorporating in Canada?
If you pay out all the profit, a corporation rarely saves much income tax: the small business rate and the dividend tax credit are designed so that the total comes close to a sole proprietor's. The saving comes from profit you leave in the corporation, taxed at a combined 9% to 12.2% in 2026 (20.5% in Quebec without 5,500 paid hours) until you take it out: that tax is deferred, not cancelled. Paid only in dividends, you also stop paying CPP, which means a smaller pension.
Should I pay myself a salary or dividends?
A salary is deducted from the corporation's income and taxed as employment income. It carries CPP (QPP in Quebec) on both the employer and employee sides, earns CPP pension and creates RRSP room. Dividends are paid from profit after corporate tax and get the dividend tax credit; they carry no CPP and create no RRSP room. As a shareholder controlling more than 40% of the voting shares, you pay no EI on your salary. Try several salaries in the calculator.
What is a personal services business?
If you would be your client's employee without the corporation (same hours, tools and control as an employee), your corporation may be a personal services business. It then loses the small business rate, pays an extra 5% federal tax and can deduct almost nothing but your salary. The calculator doesn't cover this case.
Why is Quebec different?
Quebec's small business deduction requires the corporation to pay its employees for at least 5,500 hours of work a year (partly between 5,000 and 5,500 hours). Most one-person corporations don't reach it and pay the 11.5% general Quebec rate on top of the 9% federal rate. A salary also costs the corporation the employer QPIP premium and the Health Services Fund contribution (1.65%).
Why are Saskatchewan and Nunavut missing?
Their 2026 dividend tax credit rates for non-eligible dividends couldn't be confirmed on an official source. The comparison will be added once they are; sole proprietor figures are still shown.
What isn't included?
Dividends paid to family members (tax on split income), investment income earned inside the corporation, incorporation and accounting costs (enter them as extra costs), provincial payroll taxes on large payrolls, workers' compensation, Quebec's labour standards contribution and the Health Services Fund contribution of individuals, low-income tax reductions, the alternative minimum tax, eligible dividends, and a salary combined with self-employment income.
