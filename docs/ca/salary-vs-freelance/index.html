How the comparison works

On the employee side, the calculator starts from your salary and takes off your share of the CPP (or QPP and QPIP in Quebec), your EI premiums and federal and provincial income tax, with the credits for these contributions and the Canada employment amount. Your employer's share is paid on top of your salary: it isn't part of your income.

On the self-employed side, it looks for the revenue that leaves the same income, after your expenses, both halves of the CPP (or QPP, plus the QPIP) and income tax on your net business income. Divided by your billed hours, it gives the equivalent hourly rate. The self-employed tax calculator shows the full detail, and the sole proprietor vs corporation comparison what changes with a corporation.

Frequently asked questions

How much do I need to bill to replace my salary?
With a $70,000 salary in Ontario, you keep $53,786 after CPP, EI and income tax. Self-employed with no expenses, you need $73,392 in revenue to keep the same amount, or $56.98 an hour over 1,288 billed hours.
Why does self-employment take more revenue than the salary?
Because you pay both the employee and the employer halves of the CPP (or QPP) on your net income, and you don't get the Canada employment amount. On the other hand, you don't pay EI premiums unless you opt in for special benefits. Holidays, sick days and time spent finding clients aren't paid either: the calculator divides the revenue you need by the hours you actually bill (1,288 by default).
Are employer benefits included?
Only if you enter them: group insurance, an employer pension or RRSP match and other benefits have no standard amount. Add their yearly value under “Employer-paid benefits” and the calculator raises the revenue you need to cover them.
Does it work in Quebec?
Yes: in Quebec, the calculator uses the QPP, the QPIP and the lower Quebec EI rate, and Quebec income tax. Choose your province or territory of residence: the tax and credits follow its 2026 rules.
